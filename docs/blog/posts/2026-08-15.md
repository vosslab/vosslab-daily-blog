# Retiring Compatibility Layers Without Giving Up the Editor
<!-- more -->

The interesting work today was not a new feature so much as a decision about ownership: both [Ferrum Chemical Forge](https://github.com/vosslab/ferrum-chemical-forge) and [Peptidyle](https://github.com/vosslab/peptidyle-learning-engine) moved closer to having one clear system responsible for the thing it claims to own. In Ferrum, that meant narrowing the retained desktop-editor boundary while Rust takes responsibility for chemistry and document semantics. In Peptidyle, it meant deleting shell-and-container lifecycle machinery in favor of a direct Python path that could actually be tested end to end.
## Making the boundary smaller on purpose

Ferrum Chemical Forge is the pre-alpha chemical drawing platform I am building for scientists and educators. It retains a PySide6 editor because a working desktop interaction model is valuable, but the longer-term point is durable chemistry and document ownership in Rust rather than an indefinitely enlarged compatibility layer.

Today's work closed the retained M15 utility boundary: bounded peptide insertion, conversion from a selection into a linear form, and geometry repair remain on the retained side; unused compact-sugar and descriptor-related paths do not. That distinction is unglamorous but consequential. "Keep it around just in case" is how a compatibility host quietly becomes the architecture again.

The documentation work was part of the implementation rather than an afterthought. The capability ledger now identifies which supported behavior has an accepted semantic, end-to-end, or one-time validation lane, and records refusals and dropped paths explicitly. Private-bridge tests cover artifact-provenance refusal, guarded local SVG publication, and refusal to hard-link retained origins. A chemical editor should not silently claim provenance it does not have, or let a compatibility artifact become native by implication.

Migration, interface, usage, file-format, and OASA ownership documentation were reconciled around retiring the old compatibility host. Historical provenance remains documented, but it should not be mistaken for active ownership. A migration is not complete because a replacement exists; it is complete when interfaces, tests, documentation, packaging, and the operational story agree about who owns the behavior.

## Chemistry rendered as a plan, not an accident

The more visible Ferrum work has been the Rust-owned molecule-plan grammar, `ferrum-render-plan-v2`. Rather than having every renderer rediscover geometry from a higher-level chemical model, the plan carries finite drawing primitives: `MoveTo`, `LineTo`, `CubicTo`, and `Close`, plus explicit stroke, fill, and z-order information. The same plan can flow into SVG, PNG, and the desktop-facing drawing path.

This turned out to be especially useful for ordinary bonds and Haworth projections. A normal bond is expressed in a closed presentation vocabulary such as `n1`, `n2`, and `n3`; directed solid and hashed wedges become `w1` and `h1`, with the press/start point as the tip and the release/new endpoint as the base. That is a small detail with outsized practical value: directional bonds are notoriously easy to get visually correct in one renderer and backwards in another.

The detached-ring substrate similarly chooses deliberate geometry over hand-waving. Regular carbon rings from three through eight atoms use a fixed 40-point flat-top, clockwise, y-down construction. Native `Edit -> Insert Haworth Ring...` recipes now cover alpha and beta D-glucopyranose and D-glucofuranose, with Rust owning literal C6O6 chemistry and the finite geometry that presents it.

The surprise was how often "rendering" exposed ownership questions. A front-facing `q1` or directed `w1` is not merely a prettier line; it is evidence that chemistry-facing facts can lower through a declared representation rather than leaking renderer-specific interpretation everywhere. The work is still pre-alpha, but it is starting to have a coherent answer to the question of where a drawing comes from.

## Replacing a stack with a lifecycle

Peptidyle's thread was simpler and more satisfying. The project is a mastery-learning engine: students repeatedly solve varied problems, then continue practicing fresh versions after nominal completion, while grading and answer keys remain server-side.

The accepted WP-PY-L1 cutover deletes `local_stack_control/launch.sh`, `local_stack_control/_restart.sh`, and `containers/local_identity_bootstrap.sh`. In their place is a direct, focused Python lifecycle. This was a choice against an increasingly familiar temptation: preserve a local container stack because it looks operationally serious, even when it mainly creates extra state, opaque restarts, and configuration drift.

The replacement had to earn the simplification. The final material tree recorded 4,881 passing pytest tests with no failures, skips, or warnings; all five `check_codebase.sh` stages passed; 260 checks were included in the broader verification trail. Independent repository, Podman-security, and walkthrough reviews all returned acceptance without findings.

That level of evidence matters because lifecycle simplification can otherwise be a cosmetic cleanup that leaves deployment assumptions hidden somewhere else. Here, the old machinery was removed and the direct path was exercised as the new truth.

## Where this leaves things

Both projects ended the day less interested in preserving old structures and more explicit about executable ownership. Ferrum is still consuming attention because native CDML opening, rendering, chemistry facts, and editor interoperability all meet at the boundary I am trying to shrink. Peptidyle's direct-Python cutover is accepted, so its immediate lifecycle thread is largely finished.

What has my attention next is Ferrum's open-and-render path: making the document origin, native chemistry model, and visible drawing agree without reintroducing a compatibility system by another name.
