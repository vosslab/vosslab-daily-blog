# Making Learning Systems Clearer-and Building a Game About Cancer Defense
<!-- more -->

I spent much of today making software answer a deceptively simple question more reliably: who is allowed to see, do, or change something? That work shaped [Peptidyle Learning Engine](https://github.com/vosslab/peptidyle-learning-engine), a platform for mastery learning, and it also informed a new browser game, [Attack on Cancer](https://github.com/vosslab/attack-on-cancer), where clear choices matter more than elaborate simulation.
## One source of truth for student access

Peptidyle lets students solve fresh versions of problems until they can demonstrate consistent understanding. Because grading, answer keys, feedback, and class information must be handled carefully, access rules are part of the learning experience-not just a technical safeguard.

I consolidated those rules around one central policy decision. Instead of letting rosters, group memberships, assignment settings, and old summary records each provide competing answers, I made the system evaluate the current situation through a single authority.

That means a student's access can change when it should. If an instructor narrows an assignment's audience or a student's membership is revoked, that change affects assignment lists, problem attempts, feedback, summaries, and related routes consistently. The system no longer relies on a large pile of pre-created enrollment records that may become stale.

I also preserved a useful distinction between the present and the past. A student's current eligibility can change, but a completed attempt should retain a trustworthy record of the rules that applied at the time. I used sealed, append-only receipts for that historical record, rather than allowing later membership changes to rewrite the past.

The result is more deliberate than convenient. A single policy decision requires better inputs and cleaner ownership of data. But it keeps the complexity in one visible, testable place rather than scattering exceptions through every screen and service.

## Feedback should arrive on purpose

I also refined how Peptidyle decides what students may learn from an assignment and when. Scores, correctness, written feedback, solutions, and class statistics now follow separate assignment-level timing rules.

Those categories look similar from the outside, but they answer different educational questions. An instructor may want students to know whether an answer is correct immediately, while withholding a full solution until later. Class statistics may need to wait until enough evidence exists to avoid revealing too much about a small group.

I treated the student-facing response as a carefully limited view rather than a copy of instructor data with a few fields hidden. The student interface receives only what it needs. It does not receive internal timing rules, administrative context, or raw records and then decide what to conceal in the browser.

That design also improves ordinary communication. "No score yet," "not enough information," and "this information is withheld" are different states. When software keeps those meanings distinct, it can give students a clearer answer without exposing information accidentally.

## Putting the durable rules in Rust

I saw the same design pattern in [Ferrum Chemical Forge](https://github.com/vosslab/ferrum-chemical-forge), my pre-alpha chemical drawing platform. I moved coordinate conversion rules into its Rust geometry layer, where the application can treat them as one durable rule instead of allowing multiple layers to calculate the same thing differently.

I also expanded Ferrum's command-line tools so people can inspect, validate, rewrite, and render chemical documents more directly. Alongside the larger architecture work, I cleaned up action names, keyboard behavior, application labels, documentation, and automated checks.

Much of that work is invisible when everything functions. That is the point. A scientific drawing tool should make its rules dependable enough that users do not have to wonder which layer is really in charge.

## A game needs readable choices

Late in the day, I began Attack on Cancer, a bright tower-defense game for students and curious players. It is designed around readable treatment choices, not clinical predictions or a test of memorized biology.

I kept the first version bounded: a Skin Tissue route, five treatments, five enemy types, and fifteen manual waves. I experimented with a large final enemy that pulsed, shed smaller cells, and broke apart. Then I simplified its motion to a clear double heartbeat. More animation was not automatically more useful; players need to understand what they are seeing while making decisions.

I added a second scene, Cluster Corridor, with a winding route, denser waves, and a tighter in-game economy. Lower starting resources, smaller rewards, and partial refunds make treatment purchases meaningful. The game also keeps a faster simulation-speed option for routine cleanup without removing the need to make tradeoffs.

I ended the day with two connected projects: one makes learning-system authority more explicit, and the other asks whether a game can make strategy feel clear without pretending to model medicine. In both cases, I found that the useful work was restraint: deciding what belongs in the system, what must remain distinct, and what a person can understand at a glance.
